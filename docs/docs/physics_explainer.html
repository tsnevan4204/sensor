Layered Wall DDEC

The 1D toy problem drawn out, then the physics needed to build a data-driven exterior calculus model of it, derived from Maxwell's equations step by step, with what each step means physically.

01The physical setup

A radar faces a stud wall at normal incidence. It sends a sweep of frequencies, and every interface inside the wall sends part of the wave back. The radar records the sum of those echoes as one complex number per frequency.

Measured

  • \(y(f_m)\), one complex number per frequency, \(m=1\dots 256\)
  • \(y(f) = A(f)\,\Gamma(f;\vartheta) + n(f)\)
  • Γ is the wall's reflection coefficient: echo amplitude and phase relative to what was sent

Recovered

  • Thicknesses \(t_i\)
  • Permittivity \(\varepsilon_i\) or a profile \(\varepsilon(z)\): what the material is
  • Conductivity \(\sigma_i\): how lossy it is
  • Water fraction \(\theta(z)\): moisture

Nuisance, also estimated

  • \(A(f)\): unknown gain and phase ripple of the radar hardware
  • Noise level
  • Exact standoff distance

02Maxwell's equations, split into two kinds of law

Written with differential forms, Maxwell's equations separate cleanly into laws that only count and laws that measure:

\[ \underbrace{dE = -\partial_t B,\qquad dH = \partial_t D + J}_{\text{topological: exact on any mesh}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{D = \star_\varepsilon E,\quad B = \star_\mu H,\quad J = \star_\sigma E}_{\text{metric: where the material lives}} \]

The left pair are Faraday's and Ampère's laws. They say how much flux passes through a surface given the circulation around its edge. They involve no lengths, angles or materials, only which edge bounds which face. The exterior derivative \(d\) becomes an incidence matrix of 0s and ±1s on a mesh, with no approximation.

The right three are constitutive laws. They say how strongly a given field polarizes or magnetizes this particular material, over this particular length. They need distances and material constants, and they are the only place approximation enters.

In physics terms. Faraday's law is bookkeeping for magnetic flux: a changing flux through a loop produces a voltage around it. Ampère's law is bookkeeping for current and charge. These hold for any material. The constitutive laws describe the material: drywall, wet fiberglass and air obey the same Faraday and Ampère laws and differ only in ε, μ and σ. Recovering what is behind the wall means recovering the constitutive laws. In DDEC, those are exactly the Hodge stars, the part you learn.

03Forms, cochains and the de Rham complex

A k-form is a field that is meant to be integrated over k-dimensional things: a 0-form is read at points, a 1-form along curves, a 2-form over surfaces, a 3-form over volumes. Each electromagnetic field has a natural type, and the units tell you which:

FieldUnitsPer…TypeWhat you physically measure
EV/mlength1-formvoltage along a path: \(\int_C E\cdot dl\), what a voltmeter's two probes read
BWb/m² (T)area2-formmagnetic flux through a loop: \(\iint_S B\cdot dA\), what a pickup coil responds to
HA/mlength1-formcirculation around a loop, equal to the current it encloses
DC/m²area2-formelectric flux through a surface, equal to the charge it encloses
JA/m²area2-formcurrent through a surface
ρC/m³volume3-formcharge inside a volume

The exterior derivative \(d\) takes a k-form to a (k+1)-form. In 3D it is grad, then curl, then div. Its one defining property is Stokes' theorem, \(\int_S d\omega = \oint_{\partial S}\omega\): the integral of \(d\omega\) over a piece equals the integral of \(\omega\) over that piece's boundary. Applying \(d\) twice gives zero (curl grad = 0, div curl = 0), because a boundary has no boundary.

Chaining the spaces with \(d\) gives the de Rham complex. Maxwell's equations use two of them, one for E and B and one for H, D and J, joined by the Hodge stars:

What a cochain is

A computer cannot store a field at every point. Instead, store its integral over each mesh piece of the matching dimension. That list of numbers is a cochain. A k-form becomes a k-cochain: one number per vertex, edge, face or cell. On one triangle:

Because the stored numbers are integrals, Stokes' theorem makes \(d\) exact on cochains. The voltage along an edge from a potential is the difference of its endpoint values, and the circulation around the face is the signed sum of its edges (e₁₃ counts negative because it points against the counterclockwise loop):

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}e_{12}\\ e_{23}\\ e_{13}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\0&-1&1\\-1&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_1\\ \varphi_2\\ \varphi_3\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathsf D_0:\ \text{grad}}, \qquad \underbrace{\oint_{\partial f} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e_{12}\\ e_{23}\\ e_{13}\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathsf D_1:\ \text{curl}}, \qquad \mathsf D_1\mathsf D_0 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

The last identity is curl grad = 0, holding exactly as integer arithmetic. Faraday's law on this mesh is \(\dot f = -\mathsf D_1\,\mathbf e\), with no error at any resolution. Error only enters when you need ε or μ, because \(\int E\cdot dl\) along an edge does not tell you \(\iint D\cdot dA\) through a surface without knowing the material and the geometry. That conversion is the Hodge star, and it needs the second, dual mesh.

In physics terms. E and B are what a voltmeter and a pickup coil read. H, D and J are what Ampère's and Gauss's laws count: enclosed current and enclosed charge. The two families are bookkept on two interleaved meshes, and the material is the exchange rate between them.

04Reduce to one dimension

At normal incidence on planar layers, nothing varies in x or y. Take \(\mathbf E = E_x(z,t)\,\hat x\) and \(\mathbf H = H_y(z,t)\,\hat y\). The curl equations \(\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\mu\partial_t\mathbf H\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf H = \varepsilon\partial_t\mathbf E + \sigma\mathbf E\) keep one component each:

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf E)_y = \partial_z E_x \;\Rightarrow\; \partial_z E_x = -\mu\,\partial_t H_y, \qquad (\nabla\times\mathbf H)_x = -\partial_z H_y \;\Rightarrow\; -\partial_z H_y = \varepsilon\,\partial_t E_x + \sigma E_x . \]

Why the form degree drops by one

Since nothing changes along x, take a strip of unit width in x and look at it edge-on along z. E is a 1-form, so its natural number is a line integral. Integrate along the unit x-segment ("rung") at height \(z_i\): \(e_i=\int_0^1 E_x\,dx = E_x(z_i)\). That rung sits at the single point \(z_i\) on the z-axis, so on the line \(e_i\) looks like a value at a node: a 0-cochain.

B is a 2-form, so its natural number is a flux. Integrate over the panel between two rungs: \(b_{i+\frac12}=\int_{z_i}^{z_{i+1}}\!\!\int_0^1 B_y\,dx\,dz\). That panel spans the interval \([z_i,z_{i+1}]\), so on the line \(b\) belongs to an edge: a 1-cochain. The fixed x-direction uses up one dimension of every form.

Faraday on one upper panel. Walk around the panel between \(z_i\) and \(z_{i+1}\). The two rungs contribute \(e_{i+1}\) and \(-e_i\). The two sides running along z contribute nothing, because E has no z-component. So the circulation is \(e_{i+1}-e_i\), and Faraday says it equals \(-\dot b_{i+\frac12}\). That is exactly the 3D triangle calculation above, applied to a long thin rectangle.

Ampère on one lower panel. Walk around the dual panel around node i. Its rungs carry \(h_{i+\frac12}\) and \(h_{i-\frac12}\), and Ampère says the circulation equals the rate of change of electric flux through the panel plus the current through it: \(\dot d_i + j_i\).

Why H, D and J are needed at all

Faraday alone only tells you how E changes B. A wave also needs the reverse: how B changes E. That is Ampère's law, and Ampère's law is written in terms of H (circulation) and D and J (flux and current through a surface). So one time step goes around a loop:

\[ \mathbf e \xrightarrow{\ \text{Faraday, exact}\ } \mathbf b \xrightarrow{\ \star_{\mu^{-1}}\ } \mathbf h \xrightarrow{\ \text{Ampère, exact}\ } \mathbf d,\ \mathbf j \xrightarrow{\ \star_\varepsilon^{-1},\ \star_\sigma\ } \mathbf e \;\longrightarrow\;\cdots \]

You could substitute \(H=B/\mu\), \(D=\varepsilon E\) and \(J=\sigma E\) and write everything in E and B alone. That is what the usual second-order wave equation does. But then ε and μ end up inside the curl equations, and those equations stop being exact on a mesh. Keeping H, D and J as separate unknowns is what isolates all the material into the two vertical arrows.

Check with a plane wave \(E_x = e^{j(\omega t - kz)}\). The first curl equation gives \(-jkE = -j\omega\mu H\), so \(H = E/\eta\) with \(\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}\), the wave impedance. A dense or wet material has low η.

In physics terms. Both \(E_x\) and \(H_y\) point along the layer interfaces (the interfaces are planes z = const), so both are continuous across every interface. Only their slopes jump. That is why the simple cell averages in section 06 work.

05Discretize the topological laws exactly

Faraday. Integrate \(\mu\,\partial_t H_y = -\partial_z E_x\) over primal edge \([z_i, z_{i+1}]\). The fundamental theorem of calculus turns the right side into an endpoint difference, with no approximation:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\,b_{i+\frac12} = -\,(e_{i+1}-e_i) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{\mathbf b} = -\,\mathsf D_0\,\mathbf e, \qquad \mathsf D_0\in\{0,\pm1\}^{N\times(N+1)}. \]

Ampère. Integrate \(\varepsilon\,\partial_t E_x + \sigma E_x = -\partial_z H_y\) over dual cell \([z_{i-\frac12}, z_{i+\frac12}]\):

\[ \dot d_i + j_i = -\,(h_{i+\frac12}-h_{i-\frac12}) = (\mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\mathbf h)_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot{\mathbf d} + \mathbf j = \mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\mathbf h . \]

The dual derivative is the transpose of the primal one, with a sign. That transpose is what makes the scheme conserve energy in section 07.

In physics terms. Faraday: the magnetic flux in a strip changes at a rate set by the voltage difference across its ends. Ampère: the electric flux in a cell changes, or current flows, at a rate set by the difference in H at its two faces. Both are flux bookkeeping: what enters a cell either stays or leaves. Nothing about the wall has been assumed yet.

06The Hodge stars: the only approximation, and the material

To close the system, relate the fluxes (b, d, j) to the point values (e, h). Since \(E_x\) and \(H_y\) are continuous through each cell, pull them out of the integrals:

\[ d_i=\!\int_{\tilde c_i}\!\varepsilon E\,dz \approx \underbrace{\bar\varepsilon_i\,\tilde\ell_i}_{(\star_\varepsilon)_{ii}} e_i, \qquad h_{i+\frac12}\approx \underbrace{\frac{1}{\bar\mu_{i+\frac12}\,\ell_{i+\frac12}}}_{(\star_{\mu^{-1}})_{i+\frac12}} b_{i+\frac12}, \qquad j_i \approx \underbrace{\bar\sigma_i\,\tilde\ell_i}_{(\star_\sigma)_{ii}} e_i , \]

where \(\bar\varepsilon_i\) is the arithmetic mean of ε over the dual cell, and similarly for μ and σ. Arithmetic averaging is the right choice for fields tangential to the layers; normal fields would need harmonic averaging. A cell that straddles the drywall–insulation interface gets a mix of both.

Putting it together gives the semi-discrete model:

\[ \boxed{\;\dot{\mathbf b} = -\mathsf D_0\,\mathbf e, \qquad \star_\varepsilon\,\dot{\mathbf e} + \star_\sigma\,\mathbf e = \mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\,\star_{\mu^{-1}}\,\mathbf b \;+\;\text{boundary terms}\;} \]

Eliminating b gives one second-order equation:

\[ \star_\varepsilon\,\ddot{\mathbf e} + \star_\sigma\,\dot{\mathbf e} + \underbrace{\mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\star_{\mu^{-1}}\mathsf D_0}_{\mathsf K}\,\mathbf e = \text{sources}. \]
In physics terms. The wall becomes a chain of masses and springs, or equivalently an LC ladder. \(\star_\varepsilon\) is how much electric energy each cell stores (capacitance). \(\star_{\mu^{-1}}\) is the stiffness tying neighbors together (inverse inductance). \(\star_\sigma\) is friction (conductance), which turns wave energy into heat. Moisture raises a cell's capacitance, which slows the wave and strengthens reflections, and raises its conductance, which absorbs the echo from deeper layers. Those are the two signatures you invert for, and the starter code showed both: 5% wet insulation erased the back-wall echo.

07Energy: the guarantee DDEC keeps for free

Define the discrete energy and differentiate it, using the two boxed equations:

\[ W = \tfrac12\,\mathbf e^{\!\top}\star_\varepsilon\mathbf e + \tfrac12\,\mathbf b^{\!\top}\star_{\mu^{-1}}\mathbf b, \qquad \dot W = \mathbf e^{\!\top}\!\left(\mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\star_{\mu^{-1}}\mathbf b - \star_\sigma\mathbf e\right) - \mathbf b^{\!\top}\star_{\mu^{-1}}\mathsf D_0\mathbf e = -\,\mathbf e^{\!\top}\star_\sigma\mathbf e \;+\;\text{boundary power}. \]

The two coupling terms cancel exactly because the Ampère operator is the transpose of the Faraday operator. This is a discrete Poynting theorem: energy changes only through loss inside the wall and power through the two ends.

It holds for any Hodge stars, as long as they are symmetric positive (semi)definite. That gives three constraints for a learned model:

  • \(\star_\varepsilon \succ 0\) and \(\star_{\mu^{-1}} \succ 0\): materials store energy rather than create it. Parameterize the diagonal through a softplus or exponential.
  • \(\star_\sigma \succeq 0\): the wall is passive. It can absorb but never amplify.
  • For dispersive materials like water, causality: the frequency dependence must come from poles such as a Debye term, so the Kramers–Kronig relations hold automatically.
In physics terms. However badly the learned part is trained, the model can never produce a wall that reflects more energy than it receives, or an echo that arrives before the wave did. Generative fill-in approaches have no such guarantee.

08Boundaries: the radar port and the open back

The mesh ends at node 0 (the radar plane, or the wall's front face) and at node N (behind the wall). Outside each end is uniform air, where the 1D solution is known exactly. On the left, \(E = a\,e^{-jkz} + r\,e^{jkz}\) and \(H = (a\,e^{-jkz} - r\,e^{jkz})/\eta_0\), so at z = 0:

\[ H(0) = \frac{2a - e_0}{\eta_0}, \qquad H(L) = \frac{e_N}{\eta_{\text{back}}}\quad(\text{outgoing only}). \]

These enter the Ampère equation of each half-size end cell. The boundary contributes a conductance \(1/\eta_0\) on node 0, a source \(2a/\eta_0\), and \(1/\eta_{\text{back}}\) on node N. In 1D this condition is exact, not an approximation, in both time and frequency domain. The measurement is then

\[\Gamma = \frac{e_0 - a}{a}.\]

The 300 mm air gap can be meshed, or handled analytically with the factor \(e^{-2jk_0 d}\). The starter code does the latter, which is the hybrid idea in its simplest form: exact physics in air, discretized physics in the wall.

In physics terms. Open space looks like a resistor of 377 Ω to the wall. Energy that leaves never comes back, so the boundary behaves exactly like a matched load. That resistor is radiation.

09Two ways to solve it

Time domain: leapfrog

Stagger b at half steps and e at whole steps:

\[ \mathbf b^{n+\frac12} = \mathbf b^{n-\frac12} - \Delta t\,\mathsf D_0\mathbf e^{n}, \qquad \Big(\tfrac{\star_\varepsilon}{\Delta t} + \tfrac{\star_\sigma}{2}\Big)\mathbf e^{n+1} = \Big(\tfrac{\star_\varepsilon}{\Delta t} - \tfrac{\star_\sigma}{2}\Big)\mathbf e^{n} + \mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\star_{\mu^{-1}}\mathbf b^{n+\frac12}. \]

This is Yee's FDTD scheme, written as DEC. Every step is a sparse multiply plus a diagonal solve. With σ = 0 it is symplectic and conserves a discrete energy exactly over arbitrarily long runs. It is stable when \(\Delta t \le \min_i \ell_i\sqrt{\bar\varepsilon_i\bar\mu_i}\) (the CFL condition: light must not skip a cell in one step).

Water needs one extra field. The Debye model \(\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_0\big(\varepsilon_\infty + \frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{1+j\omega\tau}\big)\) corresponds to a polarization \(p_i\) on each node with \(\tau\,\dot p_i + p_i = \varepsilon_0\Delta\varepsilon\,\tilde\ell_i\,e_i\). Physically, water molecules rotate to follow the field with a lag of τ ≈ 9 ps, comparable to the 13 ps period at 77 GHz, which is why wet material is so lossy there.

Frequency domain: one sparse solve per frequency

With \(e^{j\omega t}\) dependence, \(\partial_t \to j\omega\):

\[ \big(\mathsf K - \omega^2\star_{\varepsilon}(\omega) + j\omega\star_\sigma + \mathsf B\big)\,\hat{\mathbf e} = \hat{\mathbf s}, \qquad \mathsf K = \mathsf D_0^{\!\top}\star_{\mu^{-1}}\mathsf D_0 . \]

Here \(\mathsf B\) holds the boundary terms. Dispersion is free: just use the complex \(\varepsilon(\omega)\) of wet material directly. The matrix is tridiagonal, so each frequency costs O(N). It is also indefinite: K is positive, \(-\omega^2\star_\varepsilon\) is negative, and they cancel at resonances. This is the wave-equation issue that breaks DDEC's elliptic proofs. In 1D a direct tridiagonal solve doesn't care. In 2D it becomes the open question you'd bring to Kaibo Hu.

The matrix is complex symmetric (\(\mathsf A^{\!\top} = \mathsf A\)), so the adjoint solve for gradients reuses the same factorization. One gradient of the data misfit with respect to every ε and σ costs one extra solve per frequency.

10What the data-driven part is for

The inversion chain is short:

\[ \vartheta = (t_i,\varepsilon_i,\sigma_i,\theta(z)) \;\longrightarrow\; \star_\varepsilon(\omega),\,\star_\sigma \;\longrightarrow\; \text{solve} \;\longrightarrow\; \Gamma(f) \;\longrightarrow\; \min_{\vartheta,\,A}\sum_m\big|y(f_m) - A(f_m)\,\Gamma(f_m;\vartheta)\big|^2 . \]

With a fine mesh, nothing here needs learning. Learning pays off where a fine mesh is too costly or the physics is unknown.

1. Coarse cells with a learned Hodge star. The diagonal star on a uniform mesh has a numerical dispersion relation:

\[ \omega^2 = \frac{4}{\bar\varepsilon\bar\mu\,\ell^2}\,\sin^2\!\frac{k\ell}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\delta k}{k}\approx\frac{(k\ell)^2}{24}. \]

(Derivation: insert \(e_i = e^{-jki\ell}\) into \(\mathsf K\mathbf e = \omega^2\star_\varepsilon\mathbf e\); the stencil \(2e_i - e_{i+1} - e_{i-1}\) gives \(4\sin^2(k\ell/2)\).) At 20 cells per wavelength that error is only 0.4%. But the round trip through 89 mm of insulation is about 300 radians of phase at 79 GHz, so it accumulates to more than a radian, which is enough to spoil a thickness estimate. A learned star, possibly tridiagonal rather than diagonal, fitted so a coarse cell reproduces the exact phase across the band, fixes this with a handful of parameters. That is the concrete DDEC contribution in 1D, and it carries over to 2D, where no exact transfer-matrix reference exists.

2. A learned moisture closure. Replace the fixed mixing law \(\theta \mapsto \varepsilon(\omega)\) with a small model fitted to measured samples, constrained to Debye-type poles so it stays causal and passive.

3. A learned sensor model. A low-dimensional model for A(f) (and, later, the antenna pattern), fitted from calibration scans against a metal plate, for which Γ = −1.
